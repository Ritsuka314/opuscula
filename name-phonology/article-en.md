## One name, two readings

Read <ruby lang="ja">御坂美琴<rp> (</rp><rt>みさか みこと</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby>
aloud as *Misaka Mikoto*, then as *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">Yùbǎn Měiqín</span>*. The characters have not changed,
but the name has acquired another rhythm. Japanese gives it two groups of three
morae. Mandarin gives it four syllables, two nasal endings, and a sequence of
tones that changes when spoken together. What looked like one name on the page
has become two quite different events in the mouth.

Personal names offer a small, familiar place to hear a language's sound patterns.
Names from anime, comics, games, and related media provide useful examples.
Many readers already know both forms, yet may never have asked why *Hakurei
Reimu* and *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">Bólì Língmèng</span>* feel so different. Familiarity lets us begin with
something heard and remembered, then ask which parts of that impression we can
describe.

Here, “Chinese” means **Standard Mandarin**; the Japanese discussion uses
contemporary standard Japanese, with Tokyo-type pitch accent as its reference.
These are present-day comparisons, not reconstructions of historical
pronunciation. The examples help us hear structure; they do not establish what
the characters’ creators intended.

Our main examples are 御坂美琴 from *A Certain Scientific Railgun*, 博麗霊夢
and 霧雨魔理沙 from *Touhou Project*, and the virtual singers 初音ミク and
鏡音リン. Their Japanese readings are given on the respective official pages.
([御坂美琴][misaka], [博麗霊夢][reimu], [霧雨魔理沙][marisa],
[Piapro characters][piapro])

| <span lang="ja">博麗霊夢</span> | <ruby class="name-reading" lang="ja">はくれい<rp> (</rp><rt lang="ja-Latn">Hakurei</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby> <ruby class="name-reading" lang="ja">れいむ<rp> (</rp><rt lang="ja-Latn">Reimu</rt><rp>)</rp></ruby> | 博麗靈夢 · <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">Bólì Língmèng</span> |

Two different operations are present. 御坂美琴 can be read through the
pronunciations associated with the same characters in each language. Japanese
霊 is represented here by Traditional Chinese 靈. 初音ミク additionally
involves a different written form: ミク is katakana, while 未來 is the Chinese
rendering used on [Crypton’s Traditional Chinese character page][piapro-zh],
which also supplies 鏡音鈴. We should not silently turn the Japanese name into
an all-kanji spelling and then treat that spelling as the original. The pinyin
and structural analyses in this essay are supplied here.

Nor can a character count determine the Japanese rhythm. 琴 is one Mandarin
syllable, *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">qín</span>*, but contributes two Japanese morae, こと, to *Mikoto*. 雨
contributes さめ to *Kirisame*, not an independently chosen あめ pasted after
きり. Establish the reading of the whole name before counting its sounds.

## Three meanings of “open”

A name may sound open, rounded, narrow, or resonant. These impressions become
more useful when we distinguish three different properties of its sounds.

### 四呼: the beginning of the final

Traditional Mandarin analysis describes a syllable through its initial, final,
and tone. The final may contain a medial, a main vowel, and an ending. The 四呼
classification concerns its medial, or its main vowel when there is no medial.
([Wikipedia: 四呼][sihu])

| Class | Basic criterion | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 開口呼 | No medial; main vowel other than <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">i, u, ü</span> | 沙 <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">shā</span> |
| 齊齒呼 | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">i</span> as medial or main vowel | 琴 <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">qín</span> |
| 合口呼 | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">u</span> as medial or main vowel | 未 <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">wèi</span>, final <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">uei</span> |
| 撮口呼 | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">ü</span> as medial or main vowel | 雨 <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">yǔ</span>, vowel <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">ü</span> |

Pinyin letters need interpreting. The *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">u</span>* in *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">yǔ</span>* represents <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">ü</span>; the final in
*<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">wèi</span>* belongs with *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">uei</span>*. Conversely, the *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">i</span>* in *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">shi</span>* or *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">zhi</span>* is not the
ordinary vowel of *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">mī</span>*. The finals of *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">zhi, chi, shi, ri</span>* are conventionally
classified as 開口呼. The label therefore does not mean “a sound made with the
mouth wide open,” much less “a name that feels expansive.”
([Wikipedia: Mandarin finals and their classification][finals])

### Nasal endings: how the syllable finishes

An open syllable ends in a vowel; a closed syllable has a consonantal coda. For
these Mandarin names, a useful practical distinction is whether a final ends
in the nasal consonants *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">-n</span>* or *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">-ng</span>*. Finals without these endings include both
simple vowels and sequences with vowel offglides. ([Mandarin finals][finals])

Below, **N** marks a nasal ending and **V** marks the absence of one in these
examples. V does not mean “one vowel sound”: *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">měi</span>* and *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">lái</span>* belong in this
group too. This notation records a feature of the endings without deciding
every theoretical question about glides. The separator marks a name-part boundary,
not a required pause.

| Mandarin form | Ending pattern | Nasal endings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 御坂美琴 | VN · VN | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">bǎn</span>, <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">qín</span> |
| 博麗靈夢 | VV · NN | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">líng</span>, <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">mèng</span> |
| 霧雨魔理沙 | VV · VVV | None |
| 初音未來 | VN · VV | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">yīn</span> |
| 鏡音鈴 | NN · N | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">jìng</span>, <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">yīn</span>, <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">líng</span> |

御坂美琴 repeats a vowel-final–nasal-final pattern. 博麗靈夢 concentrates
both nasal endings in its given name. 鏡音鈴 has one on every syllable, whereas
霧雨魔理沙 has none. These are concrete differences that descriptions such
as “flowing” or “contained” might respond to.

A nasal ending is not inherently ponderous. A listener may hear its resonance
as gentle or continuous. What we can directly record is where the endings
occur; their aesthetic effect still needs listening.

### Vowel aperture: what happens inside

Vowel height is another dimension. Low *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">a</span>* has a more open configuration than
high *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">i, u, ü</span>*; lip rounding is separate again. In a final such as *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">uan</span>*, a
rounded medial leads toward a low vowel before the nasal ending.
([Wikipedia: Mandarin vowels][vowels])

霧雨魔理沙 makes the distinction audible. All five syllables lack nasal
codas, but *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">wù</span>* and *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">yǔ</span>* begin the name with high rounded vowels, while *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">shā</span>*
ends it with a low vowel. Its syllable-ending pattern is uniform; its internal
vowel movement is not. Calling the whole name “open” loses precisely the
change that may make it interesting.

## Tone numbers are not a melody

Mandarin’s four full lexical tones are conventionally numbered 1–4: high level,
rising, low or dipping, and falling. The digits name categories, not four
musical pitches. Third tone often lacks its citation-form final rise in
connected speech; before another third tone it normally takes a rising
realization within a sandhi domain. ([Mandarin tone sandhi][mandarin])

| Name | Lexical tones |
| --- | --- |
| 御坂美琴 | 4–3 · 3–2 |
| 博麗靈夢 | 2–4 · 2–4 |
| 霧雨魔理沙 | 4–3 · 2–3–1 |
| 初音未來 | 1–1 · 4–2 |
| 鏡音鈴 | 4–1 · 2 |

御坂美琴 is particularly instructive. Its two third tones meet at the
surname–given-name boundary: *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">bǎn</span> | <span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">měi</span>*. When the whole name is grouped into
one sandhi domain, the first of that pair takes a rising realization, yielding
the familiar shorthand **4–2–3–2**. With a sufficiently strong break after
the surname, the grouping changes. The pinyin above records lexical tones;
it is not a narrow transcription of every utterance.

博麗靈夢 offers a different repetition: **2–4 | 2–4**. The same rise–fall
categories recur across both name parts, even though the nasal endings occur
only in the second part. To my ear, this makes the Mandarin name feel more
paired and recurrent than its Japanese counterpart. That is an interpretation
of the pattern, not a measurement of its beauty.

初音未來 begins with two high-level tones and then moves through a falling
and a rising tone. It has a clear lexical change of contour, yet we still need
an utterance to know its actual timing and pitch range. A quiet introduction,
a surprised call, and a sung lyric do not deliver the same name in the same
way. Tone is part of the name; intonation is part of what a speaker does with it.

## What counts as length in Japanese?

The Japanese **mora**, or 拍, is a unit of phonological quantity. A short vowel
with an optional preceding consonant normally counts as one. A long vowel
counts as two; the moraic nasal ん and the first part of a geminate consonant,
written っ, each contribute a mora. Small ゃ・ゅ・ょ combines with its
preceding kana: きゃ is one mora, while きや is two. These distinctions appear
in [NINJAL’s mora inventory][ninjal].

| Name | Mora division | Count |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Misaka Mikoto | <span lang="ja">み・さ・か ｜ み・こ・と</span> | 3+3 |
| Hakurei Reimu | <span lang="ja">は・く・れ・い ｜ れ・い・む</span> | 4+3 |
| Kirisame Marisa | <span lang="ja">き・り・さ・め ｜ ま・り・さ</span> | 4+3 |
| Hatsune Miku | <span lang="ja">は・つ・ね ｜ ミ・ク</span> | 3+2 |
| Kagamine Rin | <span lang="ja">か・が・み・ね ｜ リ・ン</span> | 4+2 |

*Rin* is a useful counterexample to counting either vowels or syllables as
though they were morae. リン is normally one syllable but two morae: the nasal
counts. *Miku* also has two morae, distributed over two short syllables. Equal
mora counts leave them with different internal structures.

Likewise, *Hakurei Reimu* and *Kirisame Marisa* are both 4+3. The written れい
in the former may be realized with a long e vowel; its two-mora quantity
remains. The latter consists of seven short consonant–vowel syllables. A
repeated long-vowel sequence and a succession of short syllables can therefore
occupy the same number of morae.

[Kubozono’s account of mora and syllable][mora] explains why Japanese needs
both kinds of structure. A mora count describes phonological quantity, not
an instruction to pronounce every unit for exactly the same number of
milliseconds. Nor does the absence of a full voiced vowel necessarily remove
a mora: [vowel devoicing][ninjal] is part of Japanese speech, not evidence
that the rhythm has simply disappeared.

## Listen for what repeats

*Misaka Mikoto* begins both name parts with み. Its broad vowel pattern is:

> **i–a–a | i–o–o**

The two groups have matching length, matching initial *mi*, and two repeated
vowels at their ends. The symmetry is audible without assigning any particular
pitch accent. Mandarin *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">Yùbǎn Měiqín</span>* does not preserve that pattern, although
its **VN | VN** endings create another one.

*Kirisame Marisa* has the broad vowel sequence:

> **i–i–a–e | a–i–a**

It also repeats *ri* and *sa* across its two parts. These echoes are a more
specific observation than calling the name merely “musical.” In Mandarin,
the same characters create a quite different sequence of vowels and tones.
Written continuity preserves neither every sound nor every kind of repetition.

A vowel list is still a reduction. It omits consonants, treats vowels at a
broad level, and tells us little about pitch or delivery. Its value is that
it lets us point to one pattern at a time, then return to the whole name to
hear how the patterns work together.

## Beyond the romanization

Tokyo-type word accent concerns the presence and location of a lexical pitch
fall, including a distinction between accented and unaccented words. It is
not a Mandarin-style tone independently attached to each character, and it
is not English-style stress. Knowing that a name is 4+3 morae does not yet
tell us its accent. ([Mora, syllable, and accent][mora])

The mora divisions above consequently carry no invented accent marks. To
study a character’s performed accent, we would need to identify the speaker
and context in actual dialogue. A shout, a song, and a neutral introduction
are different evidence. OJAD can help visualize prosody, but its documentation
describes the output for arbitrary text as an **estimate**. An automatically
generated contour does not settle how a particular character name is used.
([OJAD’s functions][ojad])

Romanization also encourages comparisons between letters that represent
different sounds. In Japanese *Marisa*, *sa* is さ; in Mandarin *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">Mólǐshā</span>*,
*<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">sh</span>* marks a retroflex sibilant. Mandarin *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">q</span>* in *<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">qín</span>* marks an aspirated
alveolo-palatal affricate, not the sound an English reader might assign to q.
These articulatory distinctions are described in [Wikipedia’s account of
Mandarin consonants][consonants].

Retaining the characters therefore need not retain a name’s texture. A useful
comparison follows consonants, vowels, duration, and pitch together, while
allowing the writing to show us which identity the pronunciations share.

## Sound and association

It is tempting to take a familiar heroine’s name, notice its high vowels or
short ending, and conclude that those sounds create femininity or cuteness.
But the name comes with a voice, a face, a story, and repeated exposure. Our
response to the sound is already mixed with our knowledge of the character.

Research can identify naming conventions without reducing them to universal
properties of vowels. Barešová, Nakaya, and Matlach’s study of contemporary
Japanese given names finds associations between gender categories and
features such as name endings; the final vowel alone is an inadequate
explanation. A familiar sequence can acquire an association through usage.
That does not make it intrinsically feminine, or tell us the identity of
every person who bears it. ([Gender-specific features in contemporary Japanese
names, 2024][names])

These characters do not constitute a representative sample of Japanese names,
and phonology alone cannot explain their popularity. They do let us ask
better questions. Does the repeated *mi* in *Misaka Mikoto* contribute to the
impression? Does the contrast between the nasal endings of 靈夢 and the vowel
endings of 魔理沙 matter to a Mandarin listener? Would somebody unfamiliar
with the characters describe them the same way? The first two questions
concern audible structure; the third begins to separate sound from learned
affection.

A practical listening exercise is to compare the same name in ordinary
introductions in both languages. Mark its boundaries, Mandarin lexical tones
and endings, and Japanese morae. Then listen for what those notations omit:
consonants, actual pitch, pace, and delivery. Describe the difference before
judging it.

御坂美琴 does not need the same symmetry in both languages to remain
recognizable. *Misaka Mikoto* repeats its opening and its three-mora shape;
*<span lang="zh-Latn-pinyin">Yùbǎn Měiqín</span>* repeats its ending pattern while negotiating adjacent third
tones. One written name can support more than one kind of coherence.
Learning to hear those different kinds is where the phonology becomes
interesting.
