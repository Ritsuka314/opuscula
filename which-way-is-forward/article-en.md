## Forward to the later part

Halfway through a meeting, someone says, “We discussed that earlier; we’ll return
to it later.” In Mandarin, the familiar words are 前面, the “front part,” and
後面, the “back part.” Nobody needs to turn around. The front is what we have
already covered; the back is what we have yet to reach.

A little later, the same speaker says, “Let’s leave the past behind and look
forward.” Mandarin has 向前看, too. The front, which just held the past, now holds
the future.

A recording makes the trick especially neat:

> 往前快進一點，聽聽後面的內容。<br>
> Fast-forward a little and listen to the later part.

The instruction is perfectly usable. But line up the Chinese direction words
and it becomes peculiar: why does going *forward* take us to the *back part*?

Reverse the instruction: go back a little and replay the earlier passage.
Again, everyone knows what to do. Language performs the switch so smoothly
that it takes a moment to notice anything has changed.

<!-- section: queue-and-road -->
## A queue of events, a road to walk

Two small scenes help separate the directions.

In the first, events form a queue: opening remarks, a report, discussion, and
adjournment. The opening comes first; adjournment comes last. Mandarin 前言
(preface), 後記 (afterword), 前任 (predecessor), and 後任 (successor) fit this
picture. Front and back locate members of an ordered sequence.

In the second, we walk along a road. The stretch already travelled lies behind
us; the stretch still to come lies ahead. We can 回首往事, look back on the past,
or 展望前途, look ahead to our prospects. Here the directions belong to the
person facing along the road. Linguists distinguish reference to another event
from reference to the self. ([Yu, 2012][yu])

Return to the recording. Its “back part” has a position in the whole sequence;
“fast-forward” moves the playback position in its normal direction of travel.
A single sentence can use both arrangements.

There is another distinction hiding inside “past” and “future.” Tomorrow, we
can eat **before** the meeting. Yesterday, we may have talked **after** it.
Before tomorrow’s meeting is still in the future; after yesterday’s meeting is
still in the past. Earlier than an event and earlier than now are different
relations. One analysis of Mandarin 前 and 後 accordingly puts sequential order
at the centre of their temporal uses. ([Yang, Sinha, and Filipovic, 2023][sequence])

This lets 前年, the year before last, coexist with 前途, one’s prospects. A word
such as 前年 alone cannot tell us which way a Mandarin speaker imagines facing.
Experiments that separate event order from past–future judgments have found
support for distinct reference frames. ([Xiao, Zhao, and Chen, 2018][reference])

## Two days forward from Wednesday

Sometimes the intended frame is harder to recover. Pick a new date for this
meeting before opening the answer:

> Next Wednesday’s meeting has been moved forward two days.

Monday or Friday?

<details>
<summary>Compare the two readings</summary>
<p>Move the meeting toward the front of the schedule and it lands on Monday. Move it two days further in the direction of travel toward the future and it lands on Friday. Researchers have recorded both answers. The example shows how a single instruction can invite different interpretations.</p>
</details>

This question has been used in research on spatial and temporal reference.
The wording matters: translating it into Mandarin with 提前, “make earlier,”
already selects Monday and removes the original ambiguity.
([Bender and colleagues, 2012][meeting]; [Xiao and colleagues, 2018][reference])

Other contrasts need no ambiguous instruction. To *bring a meeting forward* is
to make it earlier; to *put the clocks forward* is to give the clocks a later
reading. ([Meeting usage][bring]; [clock usage][clock])

| Operation | Starting position | New position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bring a meeting forward an hour | 3 p.m. | 2 p.m. |
| Put a clock forward an hour | Displays 2 p.m. | Displays 3 p.m. |
| Fast-forward a recording a minute | Plays at 02:00 | Plays at 03:00 |

An appointment, a clock reading, and a playback position can all be moved.
Before comparing their directions, we need to know which one we are moving.

<!-- section: time-zones -->
## An hour ahead, an hour earlier

Arranging a call with a friend abroad, we might say 他比我們早一個小時, literally
“he is an hour earlier than us,” or 他在我們前邊, “he is ahead of us.” Suppose
we are in Hong Kong and our friend is in Tokyo. Hong Kong uses UTC+8; Japan
Standard Time is UTC+9. ([Hong Kong Observatory][hkt]; [NICT, Japan][jst])

At the same instant, our clock reads eight and his reads nine. His clock shows
the larger number and the later time. Where does “earlier” enter the picture?

Give both places the same target: nine in the morning, local time. When Tokyo
reaches nine, Hong Kong is still at eight and has an hour to wait for its own nine.

| What we compare | Hong Kong | Tokyo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clock readings at the same instant | 08:00 | 09:00 |
| Local 09:00 on the same date, expressed in UTC | 01:00 | 00:00 |

**At the same instant, it shows a later time; reaching the same clock reading,
it gets there earlier.** “Ahead” can follow the readings; “earlier” can follow
the arrival at a common mark. New Year makes this vivid: Tokyo counts down the
last ten seconds while Hong Kong still has another hour to wait.

“The time there is earlier” therefore needs context. “It is only seven there
and already eight here” compares simultaneous readings. “They welcome the new
year earlier” compares the arrivals at two local midnights. Earlier does
different work in the two sentences.

<!-- section: fast-watch -->
## A fast watch makes an early alarm

The colloquial Mandarin 錶早了, roughly “the watch is early,” invites us to
restore the missing comparison. Does it display an earlier time, or would
following it make us act too early?

Suppose it is really 8:50, but a watch already reads 9:00. It is ten minutes
fast and displays a later time. An alarm set for nine on that watch will ring
ten minutes before the correct nine. Conversely, a watch displaying 8:50 at
the real nine has an earlier reading and is ten minutes slow.

“My watch was fast, so I arrived early” and “my watch was slow, so I arrived
late” both make sense. The direction of the error in the display reverses
when we act on it. As with time zones, we can hold the instant fixed and
compare readings, or hold the reading fixed and compare when it is reached.

Even “fast” needs a scale. “Five minutes fast” describes the present error;
“gains five minutes a day” describes how the error grows. A watch set five
minutes ahead can subsequently keep perfect time and remain five minutes
fast without continuing to run faster.

<!-- section: ahead-of-schedule -->
## Arriving early, getting ahead

“She finished early, so she is ahead of schedule.” Another crossing hides in
this unremarkable sentence.

Imagine two people reading the same book. On Wednesday, Lin is at chapter
three and Zhou has reached chapter five. Chapter five comes *after* chapter
three, but Zhou is *ahead* of Lin. She has reached on Wednesday a place he will
not reach until Friday.

There are two quantities here: a date and a stage of progress. Being ahead
means **reaching a later stage at an earlier time**. As on a running track,
an earlier arrival and a greater distance travelled can describe the same
achievement.

“Ahead of its time” extends the route further. We expect a technology to become
common in the future; someone produces it today, and we call it ahead of the
age. Mandarin 思想超前 and 技術落後 do similar work for ideas and technology.
Direction acquires an evaluation: calling something “behind” often carries
an assumption about where we think it ought to go.

<!-- section: several-orders -->
## The prequel written afterwards

Stories add more sequences. Imagine an author writing a character’s adult
adventures, then returning years later to write about her childhood. The new
book is a prequel: later in publication, earlier in the character’s life.

A reader might begin with the childhood book, then read the adult adventures.
The reading order now differs from the publication order. We can arrange at
least three things in sequence: the publication of the books, the events they
narrate, and the reader’s encounters with them.

A film can do the same within one work. At minute ten, the character is an
adult; at minute twenty, a flashback takes us to childhood. We fast-forward
to a later part of the film and arrive at an earlier part of the story. “Skip
ahead” is easy for the player to carry out. “See what happens later” leaves a
question: later in the telling, or later in the character’s life?

Forward, later, and earlier can all fit the same move. Each points to a
different ordering.

<!-- section: browser-history -->
## Forward to somewhere you have been

A browser puts several of these orders behind two small arrows.

Suppose we visit pages A, B, and C, then press Back to return to B. Pressing
Forward takes us to C again. The page “ahead” is somewhere we have already been.

Think of the stored history as a row of positions: A → B → C. The selected
position moves from C to B and back to C. Meanwhile, our actual experience
unfolds as A, B, C, B, C. We can move either way through the stored history;
each new click still follows the previous one.

Undo and Redo have the same charm. Redo restores a state the document has
already had, but the act of restoring it is new. The computer lets us select
an earlier state again, in the present.

The Forward button can therefore take us back to familiar ground.

<!-- section: other-directions -->
## Other places to put time

Once we start looking, more spatial words appear.

Mandarin 上個月 and 下個月 put last month “up” and next month “down.” 遠古 and
遙遠的未來 make both antiquity and the future distant. “The holiday is
approaching” sets the event in motion; “we’re approaching the end of the year”
sets us moving instead. Even 眼前, “before the eyes,” can hold either an
immediate problem or a childhood scene appearing in memory.

Direction, distance, movement, and visibility each contribute something. The
front–back axis is one arrangement among several, though a particularly easy
one to take for granted.

Aymara offers a revealing comparison. Núñez and Sweetser identified a pattern
in language and gesture placing the past in front of the person and the future
behind. One explanation they discuss connects the known past with what is
visible in front, and the unknown future with what cannot be seen behind.
Visibility supplies a different basis for the arrangement. A future in front
of us begins to look like one possibility among others.
([Núñez and Sweetser, 2006][aymara])

In such comparisons, both words and gestures matter. A couple of expressions
alone give us too little evidence to draw one shared map of time for everyone
who speaks a language.

## Now look back at the earlier part

By now, the opening of this essay lies behind you in the experience of reading.
To revisit it, you can scroll up or choose an earlier heading in the contents.
Then you can move forward again, reading down toward the later passages.

A small journey through a page recruits several directions. Usually they
cooperate so well that we hardly stop to notice which events form the sequence,
which position is moving, or whose way of facing supplies the orientation.

At the next meeting, someone may say, “Earlier we looked back at the past;
later we’ll look forward.” The Mandarin version neatly puts 前面, 後面, and
向前看 into one sentence. Perhaps it will distract you for a second. The
speaker has not turned around. A few ordinary words have quietly changed the
reference frame.

As for further examples—we can get to those later. 後面再說。
